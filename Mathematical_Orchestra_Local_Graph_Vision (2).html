    What am I looking at?
        
    Frame and resolution. The FFT turns N samples into N/2+1 frequency bins spaced fs/N apart. A longer frame gives finer frequency resolution but blurs how the sound changes in time; a short frame does the opposite.
    
        
    Amplitude and phase. Each bin k is the sinusoid Ak cos(2π fk t + φk) with fk = k·fs/N. The frame equals the sum of all of them (DC and Nyquist counted once, the others use two-sided symmetry). Phase matters: change it and the shape changes even though the spectrum looks identical.
    
        
    Hann window. A frame cut out of a sound has hard edges, which smear energy across bins (leakage). Hann tapers the edges to zero. The price: sinusoid peaks read about half their true amplitude, and the reconstruction matches the windowed frame, not the raw samples. This page then compares against the windowed frame and says so.
    
        
    Reduced reconstruction. With only a few components the result is a real approximation. All bins (the default) with the rectangular window reproduces the analyzed frame to numerical rounding error. If the selection is longer than the frame, only the middle frame is reconstructed; the app reports this explicitly.
    
        
    Sine waves, not instruments. The FFT describes a signal as sinusoids. It does not identify square, triangle or sawtooth waves inside a voice. Those appear in the Wave Composer as separate examples.

    Why do harmonics and phase matter?
            
    Square: only odd harmonics, amplitudes 4/(πk). Triangle: odd harmonics falling as 1/k² with alternating signs. Sawtooth: all harmonics falling as 1/k with alternating signs. Phase sets where each sine starts: shift the phases of the harmonics and the same spectrum gives a very different shape. Near the jump of a square or sawtooth the partial sums overshoot by about 9% however many harmonics you add (the Gibbs phenomenon).

    Reading a spectrogram
    Time runs left to right, frequency bottom to top, and colour is amplitude. A short window shows when things happen but blurs frequency; a long window resolves close frequencies but smears timing. Vowels typically show horizontal bands (harmonics of the voice, with broad resonances), while consonants such as “s” are broad bursts of high-frequency noise. This tool displays the physics; it does not recognise phonemes or words.
